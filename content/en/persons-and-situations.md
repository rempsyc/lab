**The question is who helps whom, under which conditions.** People differ in their actions, situations differ in their demands, and the same person can respond differently across situations. A useful account specifies all three.

For the SAGE Lab, this approach connects the prediction of helping with the proposed [robust prosociality framework](/concepts/robust-prosociality/): does a pattern change when costs, recipients, or demands change?

## Dispositions meet opportunities

A disposition is a relatively enduring tendency; an affordance is a possibility or demand that a situation provides. An opportunity to exploit someone, a chance to reciprocate, and dependence on an uncertain partner create different choices.

The [Thielmann, Spadaro, and Balliet meta-analysis (2020)](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000217) relates personality associations across economic games to their situational affordances. This supports specifying the interaction structure before interpreting a trait–behavior association. It does not imply that one trait predicts every kind of help.

## Useful predictor families

| Predictor family | What to examine | Interpretation limit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Other-regarding preferences and personality | Allocation preferences, non-exploitation, cooperation, and responses to unfairness | A game outcome is not a direct reading of altruistic motivation. |
| Concern and appraisal | Perceived need, responsibility, emotional responses, and expected effectiveness | Noticing a need and feeling concern need not produce action. |
| Beliefs about others | Expected cooperation, reciprocity, and trustworthiness | Expectations matter differently when returns are possible or another person can exploit the actor. |
| Costs and resources | Time, money, effort, competing obligations, and practical ability | The same nominal cost can impose different burdens. |
| Recipients and social context | Group boundaries, social norms, observability, and support | Overall giving can conceal selective treatment of recipients. |

There is no universal ranking of these predictors. Compare them for a defined behavior, population, and set of conditions, using appropriate uncertainty and out-of-sample prediction where feasible.

## What the evidence shows

[Popov and Thielmann (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1111/jopy.12957) tested four proposed core tendencies across six incentivized games. Support for the predicted matches was mixed. The study is useful both as a test of person–situation reasoning and as a warning against treating the framework’s predictions as settled facts.

[Yelbuz and Thielmann (2026)](https://doi.org/10.1037/pspp0000583) distinguished general giving from selectivity across recipients, finding largely different personality correlates. Average generosity and the distribution of that generosity therefore deserve separate outcomes.

Experimental evidence also shows that a situation can change helping. In the classic [Darley and Batson study (1973)](https://doi.org/10.1037/h0034449), greater hurry reduced stopping to help. This result concerns a particular task and sample; it cannot establish that personality is generally unimportant.

## Average levels and response patterns

**Illustrative example:** two people help equally often when an action is easy. As effort increases, one reduces helping more sharply. They differ in sensitivity to effort even when they match in the easy condition.

For an intervention, distinguish an increase in average helping from a change in sensitivity to a manipulated condition. Test the interaction directly. A significant effect in one condition and a nonsignificant effect in another do not themselves demonstrate a difference.

A flatter cost response is not automatically better. The beneficiary’s actual needs, legitimate competing obligations, and the helper’s future capacity still matter.

## Designing an informative study

Sample repeated opportunities where possible. Separate variation between people from variation within a person; account for repeated observations, learning, and unequal exposure to opportunities. Define which recipients and situations the conclusions cover.

Randomizing a situational feature can support a causal claim about that feature. A measured trait’s association or moderation pattern does not show that changing the trait would change helping. Training may also alter where people go and what needs they notice, so standardized tasks and daily-life measures answer complementary questions.

Strong situational effects and modest trait correlations do not establish a universal division of explained variance. That division depends on the people, behaviors, situations, and reliability of measurement.

## Essential reading

- **Thielmann et al. (2020):** the personality–affordance synthesis.
- **Popov and Thielmann (2025):** a direct test, including predictions that received limited support.
- **Yelbuz and Thielmann (2026):** general generosity versus recipient selectivity.
- **Darley and Batson (1973):** a classic experimental manipulation of hurry.
