Start with one week in your course outline. List the readings, quizzes, and assignments. For each one, write down **what you need to be able to do** and **when you’ll do it**. Try the plan for a week, then adjust it using the time you actually spent and what you can do with the material.

## Start with a time budget

At UQAR, one credit represents about 45 hours of total work, including assessments. That’s about 135 hours for a three-credit course. If it meets three hours a week over 15 weeks, the arithmetic leaves about six hours per week outside class, averaged over the term. Use that estimate to reserve time in your calendar. Your workload will vary across weeks and courses, and your grade reflects what you demonstrate on assessments under the course’s criteria and weighting.

[UQAR Regulation 5, credit definition and section 13 on assessment and grading](https://www.uqar.ca/app/uploads/2024/04/reglement5.pdf)

## Example week in social psychology

Suppose you have 20 pages to read before class, a quiz on Friday, and a short written assignment due Monday. Here’s an example of how you could use six hours outside class during that week.

| When | Task | Time to reserve |
| --- | --- | ---: |
| Monday | Read the 20 pages. Identify the main claim and mark two ideas you want to clarify in class. | 1 h 30 |
| Wednesday and Thursday | Prepare for the quiz in two 30-minute sessions. Without your notes, explain how you’d tell conformity and obedience apart in a new scenario. Check your answer. | 1 h |
| Thursday | Read the assignment rubric, pick an example, and outline how one course concept applies to it. | 1 h 30 |
| Sunday | Draft the assignment, then revise it using the rubric. | 2 h |
| | **Total outside class** | **6 h** |

Reading for a class discussion and preparing to apply a concept can take different amounts of time. For example, Rice’s model estimates about **1½ hours** to understand 20 textbook pages with some unfamiliar concepts and **nearly 3 hours** if you also stop to work through examples, draw inferences, and evaluate claims. These are two model estimates for different reading tasks. Use your own experience with the assigned chapter to improve your estimate.

[Rice University’s estimator methodology and reading rates](https://cte.rice.edu/resources/workload-estimator)

## Adjust after your first week

1. **Compare your estimate with what happened.** If the chapter took closer to three hours, start the next reading earlier or split it across two sessions. Check your other courses for deadlines before shifting work.
2. **Check what you can do.** Close your notes and answer a practice question or apply the concept to an unfamiliar example. Spread these checks across a few days. Use quiz and assignment feedback to decide whether to change how you study, how much time you reserve, or both.
3. **Ask about the task if you’re unsure.** For a reading, you could ask whether you’re expected to explain the main argument, interpret a figure, or apply the concepts. If workload or scheduling keeps getting in the way, the Centre d’aide à la réussite (CAR) offers resources on study strategies and time management.

## Estimate a week from your course outline

The **Workload Estimator 2.0** at Wake Forest lets you try different assumptions about readings and assignments. Use its output as a first estimate, then compare it with your own experience.

Instructors can also walk students through a week of their actual course and use the exercise to check whether the work fits the time available.
