**The central problem:** when do people act to benefit others, what prevents them from doing so, and how can beneficial action be cultivated? The field studies actions, motives, relationships, and social conditions. These are connected objects of study, with different requirements for evidence.

For the SAGE Lab, this map connects established research to our proposed [robust prosociality framework](/concepts/robust-prosociality/).

## What are we trying to explain

| Concept | Central question | A distinction to retain |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Prosocial behavior | Does an action aim to benefit others or produce benefit, under the definition used? | Definitions differ about intentions, consequences, costs, and social approval. |
| Altruism | Is another’s welfare the ultimate motivational goal? | An observable benefit does not identify the motive. |
| Compassion | Does another’s suffering elicit concern and a wish to alleviate it? | Feeling concern and providing effective help are separate outcomes. |
| Cooperation | How do people contribute under interdependence? | Cooperation can also benefit the actor. |
| Effective helping | Does the response fit the recipient’s needs and improve an outcome that matters? | Frequency and sacrifice do not establish effectiveness. |

Choose and state a definition before choosing a task. The review by [Pfattheicher, Nielsen, and Thielmann (2022)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.copsyc.2021.08.021) documents variation in definitions and calls for corresponding operationalizations.

## How the field studies these questions

Researchers use resource-allocation games, actual helping opportunities, donations, volunteering, reports from participants and others, and repeated observations in daily life. Each samples particular choices and opportunities. A money allocation is evidence about that decision; extending the claim to a general disposition or everyday helping requires additional validation.

Separate **motivation, opportunity, action, and recipient outcome**. An intervention might increase concern without changing behavior, increase action without increasing benefit, or improve the choice of help without increasing its frequency. See [Measuring prosocial behavior](/concepts/measuring-prosocial-behavior/).

## What predicts behavior

A useful starting question is **which person encounters which recipient, demand, and opportunity?** Dispositions such as social preferences and personality, beliefs about others, perceived need, costs, responsibility, group membership, and available support can all enter the explanation.

A predictor’s association depends on the behavior and circumstances studied. A correlation does not establish an intervention target, and an experimentally demonstrated situational effect does not establish what share of all helping is situational. The [persons and situations primer](/concepts/persons-and-situations/) develops this logic.

## Major explanatory approaches

| Approach | Question it addresses | What it does not establish alone |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Evolutionary cooperation accounts | Under what selection and interaction conditions can cooperation persist? | The actor’s conscious motive in an episode. |
| Empathy–altruism and egoistic alternatives | Is help directed ultimately at another’s welfare, personal relief, or another goal? | Long-term generalization from one choice. |
| Identity and intergroup accounts | How do shared identities and group boundaries organize concern? | That broader identification always produces effective help. |
| Norms and moral commitments | How do expectations, responsibility, and internal obligations guide action? | That a stated value is enacted. |
| Motivation and decision accounts | How do emotions, principles, anticipated impact, and personal consequences shape choice? | A single mechanism behind every act. |
| Learning and expertise accounts | How do intentions become accessible, practiced, and adaptable responses? | That fluent repetition remains beneficial after needs change. |

Evolutionary function, immediate motivation, and development through training are different explanatory questions. The [SAVE framework](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-psych-010213-115054) connects appraisal, values, and emotions across levels. [Erlandsson and Dickert’s typology](https://doi.org/10.1177/08997640241280983) organizes proposed decision mechanisms into four families: emotions, moral principles, anticipated impact, and anticipated personal consequences. These syntheses help compare explanations.

## Landmarks and open questions

The [altruism primer](/concepts/altruism/) introduces classic time-pressure, bystander, empathy–escape, and group-identity studies. Their importance lies in the questions and contrasts they established; individual experiments do not settle every boundary condition.

Current questions include how measures generalize, which interventions change actual behavior, how concern extends across recipients, and whether benefits persist when support or incentives change. Brief interventions can test immediate mechanisms. Durability needs follow-up; transfer needs different tasks or recipients.
